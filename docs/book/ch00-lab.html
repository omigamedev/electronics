Chapter 0

Your Lab & How to Use This Book

You write software, so you already know how to debug a system you can't see. Electronics works the same way, except the state lives in electric fields instead of memory. This chapter sets up your bench, your simulators and your habits.

How this book works

Each chapter mixes four kinds of material:

  • Explanations that build intuition first and add the math only where it pays for itself. Optional deep dives sit in collapsible boxes.
  • Live widgets: plots, animated circuits and simulators you can poke. Drag every slider. Predict what will happen before you drag it.
  • Exercises as you go, Brilliant/Duolingo style. You earn ⚡ XP, and solving at least one per day keeps your 🔥 streak alive. Progress is saved in your browser.
  • Lab boxes: real builds on a breadboard, with what to measure.

Your first live circuit

Here is the "hello world" of electronics: a battery, a resistor and an LED in a loop. Click the switch. The orange dots are conventional current (positive charge flowing from + to −). We'll unpack what's actually moving in Chapter 1.

The resistor sets the current. The battery voltage minus the LED's forward voltage is what is left across the resistor, so by Ohm's law:

$$I = \frac{V_{\text{supply}} - V_{F}}{R}$$

The single most useful formula for driving LEDs from a GPIO pin.

In Figure 0.1, set the LED current between 9 mA and 11 mA with a 3.3 V supply (like an ESP32 pin), with the switch closed.
\(R \approx (3.3 - 1.9)/0.010 = 140\,\Omega\). In practice you'd pick the standard value 150 Ω. Note how little headroom 3.3 V leaves for a blue or white LED (\(V_F \approx 3\) V).
A 5 V Arduino pin drives a red LED (\(V_F = 2.0\) V) at 10 mA. What resistor do you need?
Voltage left over for the resistor = 5 − 2.0. Then \(R = V/I\), with \(I = 0.010\) A.
\(R = (5 - 2.0)/0.010 = 300\,\Omega\). The nearest standard values are 300 Ω (E24) or 330 Ω (E12). You can type answers like 300, 0.3k or 300 ohm.

Reading plots

Plots are interactive too. Hover (or touch) to read values. This one shows how the LED current from Figure 0.1 depends on the resistor for a few supply voltages. Notice the shape: current goes as \(1/R\), so doubling R halves the current.

∑ Log axes

On a log axis each grid decade (10, 100, 1k, 10k) is the same distance apart. Engineers use log axes because component values span many orders of magnitude, and because \(y = k/x\) becomes a straight line on log–log axes. See Appendix A.

Your bench

You can learn a lot in simulators, but electronics is an experimental science. These tools pay for themselves quickly:

ToolWhy you need itBuying notes
MultimeterVoltage, current, resistance, continuity (beep), diode test. Your console.log.Auto-ranging, with a fused current input. UNI-T UT61E+, Brymen BM235, or any decent mid-range meter.
Bench power supplyAdjustable voltage with a current limit. A short circuit becomes a number on a display instead of smoke.0–30 V, 0–5 A is plenty. Set the current limit low on first power-up.

Which tool would you reach for first? Sort each problem.
🔢 Multimeter
📈 Oscilloscope
🔍 Logic analyser
  • Is the 3.3 V rail actually 3.3 V?
  • Which I²C address does the sensor ACK?
  • Is there ringing on the motor PWM edges?
  • Is this wire broken? (continuity)
  • Is SPI sending the bytes my code thinks it sends?
  • How much ripple is on the buck converter output?
Rule of thumb: the multimeter is for steady values, the scope is for analog shape over time, and the logic analyser is for many digital lines and protocol decoding.

Safety, the short version

⚠️ Three rules
  1. Never work on mains (wall power). Everything in this book runs at under about 30 V DC. That's safe for your body; your parts are what's at risk.
  2. Lithium batteries store a lot of energy. A shorted LiPo can deliver over 100 A and catch fire. Use proper chargers, never puncture or over-discharge, charge on a non-flammable surface, and fuse your packs.
  3. Power new circuits from a current-limited supply before you ever connect a battery.

What actually hurts a human is current through the body. Dry skin has a resistance of maybe tens of kΩ, so at 12 V a few hundred µA flow, which you can't even feel. At 230 V you get milliamps through the chest, which can be lethal. That's Ohm's law, applied to you.

Which of these are good habits? (Select all that apply.)
  1. Set the bench supply's current limit to ~100 mA when powering a new board
  2. Leave a LiPo charging overnight on the bed
  3. Put a fuse close to the battery in a robot
  4. Double-check polarity before connecting a battery
  5. Measure current by putting the multimeter (in A mode) directly across the battery
An ammeter is nearly a short circuit. Placing it across a battery shorts the battery and usually blows the meter's fuse. Current is measured in series. Chapter 2 explains why.

Simulators

  • Falstad CircuitJS runs in the browser and animates current and voltage. It's perfect for building intuition. Many figures in this book have a Falstad equivalent you can recreate.
  • LTspice (free, Analog Devices) is the industry-standard SPICE. Use it for transient and AC analysis and for real part models.
  • KiCad (free) handles schematics and PCB layout. We'll use it in Chapter 9.
  • Wokwi simulates Arduino/ESP32 code with virtual parts in the browser. Great for testing firmware before hardware arrives.
Put the "engineer's loop" in order.
  1. Predict (math / intuition)
  2. Simulate
  3. Build on a breadboard
  4. Measure
  5. Compare and update your mental model
This is the scientific method, applied to circuits. Bugs hide in the gap between what you predicted and what you measured.

The road map

Chapters 1–3 are the physics and circuit theory. They're the part most hobbyist tutorials skip, and it's why their readers get stuck when a motor resets the microcontroller. Chapters 4–5 are the components that make robots move and stay powered. Chapters 6–7 cover talking to chips and programming them. Chapter 8 is motion and control, and Chapter 9 puts it all on your own PCB.

Software developers often compare voltage to a "pressure" and current to a "flow rate". In that analogy, what plays the role of a resistor?
  1. A pump
  2. A narrow section of pipe
  3. A water tank
  4. The water itself
A narrow pipe needs more pressure difference to push the same flow through it. A pump is the battery, a tank is roughly a capacitor, and the water is the charge. Chapter 1 shows where this analogy works and where it breaks: water doesn't create magnetic fields!

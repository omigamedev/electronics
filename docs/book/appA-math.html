Appendix A

Math Toolkit

Electronics uses a small, friendly slice of mathematics: powers of ten, exponentials, logarithms, a little calculus, sine waves, complex numbers and a matrix or two. If you've written a game loop or a physics engine, you already know most of it under different names. This appendix rebuilds each tool from intuition, with a widget to play with.

Prefixes, scientific notation and orders of magnitude

Component values in a single circuit routinely span fifteen orders of magnitude: a 10 pF capacitor, a 10 MΩ resistor, a 10 µA sleep current, a 10 A motor. Writing these as plain decimals is hopeless, so engineers use scientific notation, \(a\times10^{n}\), and its friendlier cousin, engineering notation, where \(n\) is always a multiple of 3 and gets a letter prefix:

Schematics often put the prefix where the decimal point would go, because a printed decimal point can vanish in a photocopy: 4k7 means 4.7 kΩ, 2R2 means 2.2 Ω and 1n5 means 1.5 nF. Resistors also use a three-digit code on SMD parts: 472 means \(47\times10^{2} = 4700\ \Omega\).

Rearranging formulas

An equation is a balance: whatever you do to one side, do to the other, and it stays true. Rearranging a formula to isolate an unknown is the same as inverting a function, which you've done in code many times. Each operation applied to the unknown has to be undone in reverse order, like popping a stack:

  • Something added to the unknown gets subtracted from both sides.
  • Something multiplying the unknown divides both sides.
  • A square is undone by a square root; an exponential by a logarithm (next sections).

Worked example. An LED circuit obeys \(V_s = IR + V_F\). Solve for \(R\). The unknown \(R\) is multiplied by \(I\), and then \(V_F\) is added. Undo in reverse: subtract \(V_F\), then divide by \(I\):

$$V_s - V_F = IR \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = \frac{V_s - V_F}{I}.$$

Second example. Power in a resistor is \(P = V^2/R\). What voltage gives 0.25 W in 100 Ω? Multiply both sides by \(R\) to get \(V^2 = PR\), then take the square root: \(V = \sqrt{PR} = \sqrt{25} = 5\) V. Notice the square root has two answers, ±5 V. Physics tells you which one you want: here either polarity dissipates the same power.

Always finish with a units check. If your rearranged formula for a resistance comes out in V·A, you've made a mistake: \(\text{V}/\text{A} = \Omega\), while \(\text{V}\cdot\text{A} = \text{W}\).

⚠️ Skip the "formula triangle"

Many tutorials teach a V-I-R triangle you cover with your thumb. It works for exactly one formula shape (\(a = bc\)) and teaches nothing that transfers. The two rules above work for every formula in this book, including the ones with sums, squares and exponentials.

The RC cutoff frequency is \(f_c = \dfrac{1}{2\pi RC}\). You want \(f_c = 1\) kHz with \(C = 100\) nF. Which expression gives \(R\)?
  1. \(R = 2\pi f_c C\)
  2. \(R = \dfrac{1}{2\pi f_c C}\)
  3. \(R = \dfrac{2\pi C}{f_c}\)
  4. \(R = \dfrac{f_c}{2\pi C}\)
Multiply both sides by \(R\), then divide both sides by \(f_c\): \(R = 1/(2\pi f_c C) = 1/(2\pi\cdot10^3\cdot10^{-7}) \approx 1.59\ \text{k}\Omega\). The formula is symmetric in \(R\) and \(C\), which is a good memory aid.

Exponentials, \(e\) and the time constant

Many quantities in nature change at a rate proportional to how much of them there is. Money with compound interest grows faster when there is more of it. A capacitor discharging through a resistor loses charge faster when there's more charge (higher voltage, more current). Anything with this property follows an exponential.

Where \(e\) comes from

Suppose a bank pays 100% interest per year. Compounded once, \(\$1\) becomes \(\$2\). Compounded twice (50% each half year): \(1.5^2 = 2.25\). Monthly: \((1 + 1/12)^{12} \approx 2.613\). Every second: still about 2.718. The limit as you compound more and more often is a specific number:

$$e = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1 + \frac1n\right)^n = 2.718\,281\,8\ldots$$

Euler's number. \(e^x\) is the result of growing continuously at a rate equal to your current size, for "time" \(x\).

The special property of \(e^x\) is that its slope equals its value: \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{x} = e^{x}\). That's why it solves every "rate proportional to amount" problem.

Decay and the time constant τ

A capacitor \(C\) discharging through a resistor \(R\) follows

$$v(t) = V_0\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = RC$$

Exponential decay. Charging toward \(V_s\) is the mirror image: \(v(t) = V_s\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)\).

The time constant τ sets the timescale. A handful of numbers are worth memorising:

Engineers treat 5τ as "done". Another handy fact: the initial slope of the curve, extended as a straight line, reaches the final value after exactly one τ. Exponentials never actually arrive, but after 5τ the remaining 0.7% is usually smaller than the tolerance of your components.

👩‍💻 Developer's analogy: the exponential moving average

The classic smoothing filter avg += alpha * (x - avg) is an RC low-pass filter in code. Each step closes a fixed fraction of the remaining gap, which is exactly exponential behaviour, with a time constant of about dt / alpha. You'll see this equivalence made precise in the Euler section below.

Logarithms, decibels and log axes

A logarithm answers "what power?": \(\log_{10}(1000) = 3\) because \(10^3 = 1000\). It's the inverse of the exponential, and it turns multiplication into addition:

$$\log(ab) = \log a + \log b, \qquad \log(a^n) = n\log a, \qquad \ln x = \log_e x.$$

That property is why gains in a signal chain are expressed in a log unit: cascaded amplifiers multiply their gains, and in log units you just add them. Logarithms also solve for time in exponentials. How long until an RC charge reaches 90%? Set \(1 - e^{-t/\tau} = 0.9\), so \(e^{-t/\tau} = 0.1\), and take \(\ln\) of both sides: \(t = \tau\ln 10 \approx 2.3\tau\).

Decibels

The decibel compares two powers on a log scale:

$$G_\text{dB} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_2}{P_1} = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_2}{V_1}$$

Power ratios use 10 log; voltage (or current) ratios use 20 log. They give the same dB because \(P \propto V^2\), and the square becomes a factor of 2 outside the log.

Landmarks to memorise: +3 dB ≈ double the power (×1.41 voltage); +6 dB ≈ double the voltage (×4 power); +10 dB = 10× power; +20 dB = 10× voltage (100× power). Negative dB means attenuation. A filter's cutoff frequency is defined as its −3 dB point, where the output power has dropped to half and the voltage to \(1/\sqrt2 \approx 70.7\%\).

Absolute levels use a fixed reference: dBm is power relative to 1 mW. A BLE radio transmitting at 0 dBm sends 1 mW; at +8 dBm it sends 6.3 mW; a received signal of −90 dBm is 1 picowatt. A link budget is then just addition and subtraction.

A voltage divider halves a signal's voltage. What is its gain in dB?
Voltage ratio, so use \(20\log_{10}(0.5)\).
\(20\log_{10}0.5 = 20\times(-0.301) = -6.02\) dB. Two such dividers in a row (if they didn't load each other) would give −12 dB: in dB, gains add.
At a low-pass filter's −3 dB cutoff frequency... (select all that apply)
  1. the output power is about half the passband power
  2. the output voltage is half the passband voltage
  3. the output voltage is about 70.7% of the passband voltage
  4. the signal is completely blocked
\(-3\) dB means \(10^{-3/10} \approx 0.501\) in power and \(10^{-3/20} \approx 0.708\) in voltage. Half the voltage would be −6 dB. A first-order filter keeps falling gradually beyond cutoff, at −20 dB per decade.

Derivatives and integrals, by intuition

The derivative is a rate

The derivative \(dv/dt\) is the slope of a graph at a point: how fast \(v\) changes per unit time. Numerically, it's what you get from (v[n+1] - v[n]) / dt as dt shrinks toward zero. Physically, it's a speedometer reading: the rate right now, not the average over the trip.

Two derivatives run all of electronics:

$$i_C = C\,\frac{dv}{dt}, \qquad v_L = L\,\frac{di}{dt}$$

A capacitor's current is proportional to how fast its voltage changes; an inductor's voltage is proportional to how fast its current changes.

Read them as rules about slopes. A flat voltage means no capacitor current, however high the voltage. A steep voltage edge means a big current spike. That's why a fast digital edge driving a capacitive load draws large current pulses even though the average current is tiny. You only need a few derivative rules: \(\frac{d}{dt}(at) = a\), \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-t/\tau} = -\frac1\tau e^{-t/\tau}\), and \(\frac{d}{dt}\sin\omega t = \omega\cos\omega t\).

In Figure A.5, choose the sine waveform and move the tangent point to a time where the capacitor current is zero (within 2% of its peak).
The current is zero at the peaks and troughs of the voltage, where the slope is flat. It's largest where the voltage crosses zero, where the slope is steepest. So for a capacitor, the current sine is shifted a quarter cycle (90°) ahead of the voltage. That 90° shift is what complex impedance encodes.
A 10 µF capacitor's voltage rises linearly from 0 to 5 V in 1 ms. What current flows into it during the ramp?
\(dv/dt = 5\ \text{V} / 1\ \text{ms}\). Multiply by \(C\).
\(i = 10\times10^{-6}\times 5/10^{-3} = 0.05\) A = 50 mA. Make the ramp 100× faster (10 µs) and the current is 5 A. This is why capacitive loads need inrush limiting.

The integral is accumulation

The integral is the reverse: it adds up a rate over time to get a total. If current is the rate at which charge flows, then the charge delivered between \(t_0\) and \(t_1\) is the area under the current curve:

$$Q = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} i(t)\,dt, \qquad v_C(t) = v_C(0) + \frac1C\int_0^t i\,dt$$

Numerically: Q += i * dt in a loop. This is exactly what a battery "fuel gauge" chip does with its current-sense resistor (coulomb counting).

Battery capacity in mAh is an integral too: 2000 mAh means the area under the current-vs-time curve is 2 A·h = 7200 C. The derivative and integral undo each other (the fundamental theorem of calculus), which is why \(i = C\,dv/dt\) and \(v = \frac1C\int i\,dt\) say the same thing.

A sensor draws a constant 2 mA for a 3 ms measurement burst. How much charge does it draw from its supply capacitor?
Area of a rectangle: current × time.
\(Q = 2\times10^{-3}\times3\times10^{-3} = 6\times10^{-6}\) C = 6 µC. From a 100 µF capacitor with no other supply, the voltage would drop by \(Q/C = 60\) mV. Type 6u.

Rates are slopes; totals are areas. Current is \(dQ/dt\), slew rate is \(dv/dt\), and \(v_L = L\,di/dt\). Charge used, energy \(\int P\,dt\) and capacitor voltage \(\frac1C\int i\,dt\) are accumulations.

A differential equation, solved in a loop

Combine the capacitor law with Ohm's law and you get a differential equation: an equation relating a quantity to its own rate of change. For a capacitor charged from \(V_s\) through \(R\), the current is \((V_s - v)/R\), and that current equals \(C\,dv/dt\):

$$C\frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{V_s - v}{R} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{V_s - v}{\tau}.$$

Read it as a rule: the voltage moves toward \(V_s\) at a speed proportional to how far away it is. The exact solution is the exponential from earlier, \(v = V_s(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\). But you don't need to know that to solve it. A developer can simply simulate it, and this is how SPICE and every game physics engine work at heart. Euler's method takes small time steps and uses the current slope to predict the next value:

// Forward Euler for an RC circuit
let v = 0;                  // capacitor starts empty
const tau = R * C, dt = tau / 10;
for (let t = 0; t < 5 * tau; t += dt) {
  const dvdt = (Vs - v) / tau;   // the physics: slope right now
  v = v + dvdt * dt;             // step forward along that slope
  plot(t + dt, v);
}

Rewrite the update as v += (dt/tau) * (Vs - v) and you have the exponential moving average from the earlier callout, with alpha = dt/tau. Each step closes a fraction \(dt/\tau\) of the gap, so the error shrinks by a factor \((1 - dt/\tau)\) per step. That factor tells you everything about the method's behaviour:

  • \(dt \ll \tau\): the factor is close to 1 and the result tracks the exact curve closely. The error shrinks in proportion to \(dt\) (a "first-order" method).
  • \(\tau < dt < 2\tau\): the factor is negative, so the solution overshoots and oscillates around \(V_s\), though it still converges.
  • \(dt > 2\tau\): the factor has magnitude above 1, so each step overshoots more: the simulation is unstable and blows up. The circuit didn't change; the numerics did.
In Figure A.7, find a step size where the Euler solution oscillates around the final value but still converges.
Any \(dt/\tau\) between 1 and 2 does it: the factor \(1 - dt/\tau\) is between −1 and 0, so each step overshoots by a shrinking amount. Real simulators use smarter "implicit" methods (backward Euler, trapezoidal) that stay stable at any step size, which is why LTspice doesn't explode on stiff circuits.
Run two Euler steps by hand: \(V_s = 5\) V, \(v_0 = 0\), and \(dt/\tau = 0.1\). What is \(v\) after the second step?
Step 1: \(v_1 = 0 + 0.1\times(5 - 0)\). Step 2: \(v_2 = v_1 + 0.1\times(5 - v_1)\).
\(v_1 = 0.5\) V, then \(v_2 = 0.5 + 0.1\times4.5 = 0.95\) V. The exact value at \(t = 0.2\tau\) is \(5(1 - e^{-0.2}) = 0.906\) V. Euler with a coarse step overestimates here because it uses the slope at the start of each step, which is the steepest.

Sine waves, frequency, phase and RMS

A point going round a circle at constant speed, seen from the side, traces a sine wave. Alternating voltages, radio carriers and the vibrations of a speaker are sine waves (or sums of them), so it pays to name the parts precisely:

$$v(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi), \qquad \omega = 2\pi f, \qquad T = \frac1f$$

\(A\) is the amplitude (peak), \(f\) the frequency in hertz (cycles per second), \(T\) the period, \(\omega\) the angular frequency in radians per second, and \(\varphi\) the phase in radians, which shifts the wave in time.

Why radians? Because one full cycle is \(2\pi\) radians, and with angles in radians the calculus stays clean: \(\frac{d}{dt}\sin\omega t = \omega\cos\omega t\), with no stray factors. Degrees are fine for talking (a 90° phase shift is \(\pi/2\) rad), but formulas want radians. A phase of \(\varphi\) radians is a time shift of \(\varphi/\omega\).

RMS: the "equivalent DC" value

What DC voltage would heat a resistor as much as a given AC waveform? Power is \(v^2/R\), so we need the average of \(v^2\), then take the square root to get back to volts: the root of the mean of the square, RMS.

For a sine: \(\sin^2 x = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2x)\), and the \(\cos 2x\) part averages to zero over a cycle. So the mean of \(A^2\sin^2\) is \(A^2/2\), and

$$V_\text{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^T v^2\,dt} = \frac{A}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.707\,A \quad\text{(sine only)}$$

A square wave of amplitude \(A\) has RMS \(A\); a triangle has \(A/\sqrt3\). Cheap multimeters assume a sine; "true RMS" meters really compute the integral.

Mains "230 V" is an RMS value; the peak is \(230\sqrt2 \approx 325\) V. In code, RMS is Math.sqrt(samples.reduce((s, x) => s + x*x, 0) / samples.length).

A mains sine wave peaks at 325 V. What is its RMS value?
Divide the peak by \(\sqrt2\).
\(325/1.4142 \approx 229.8\) V, which is the "230 V" on the label. It means 325 V peak across a 100 Ω heater makes the same heat as 230 V DC would.

Complex numbers and phasors

In a circuit driven by a sine wave, every voltage and current is a sine at the same frequency. Only two things differ from one signal to another: the amplitude and the phase. Complex numbers are the bookkeeping device that tracks both at once, and they turn calculus on sine waves into simple multiplication.

The imaginary unit

Define a number \(j\) with \(j^2 = -1\). (Mathematicians write \(i\); engineers use \(j\) because \(i\) is current.) A complex number \(z = a + jb\) has a real part \(a\) and an imaginary part \(b\). Draw it as a point (or arrow) in the plane, with real horizontal and imaginary vertical. Then it also has a magnitude and an angle:

$$|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \qquad \angle z = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a), \qquad z = |z|\,e^{j\angle z}$$

Rectangular and polar forms. The link is Euler's formula, \(e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta\): a unit arrow at angle \(\theta\).

The magic is in multiplication. Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their magnitudes and adds their angles. Multiplying by \(j\) is a 90° rotation (do it twice and you get \(-1\), a 180° turn, consistent with \(j^2 = -1\)).

Phasors: sine waves as rotating arrows

A sine wave \(A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\) is the shadow (real part) of an arrow of length \(A\) rotating at \(\omega\) and starting at angle \(\varphi\): \(\operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j\varphi}e^{j\omega t}\}\). Every signal in the circuit rotates at the same \(\omega\), so we can drop the shared \(e^{j\omega t}\) and keep just \(A e^{j\varphi}\), the phasor. Adding sines of the same frequency becomes adding arrows tip-to-tail, far easier than trigonometric identities.

Impedance

Here's the payoff. For a phasor current \(I e^{j\omega t}\), differentiating just multiplies by \(j\omega\). So \(v = L\,di/dt\) becomes \(V = j\omega L\,I\), and \(i = C\,dv/dt\) becomes \(I = j\omega C\,V\). Each component obeys a complex Ohm's law, \(V = ZI\), with an impedance:

$$Z_R = R, \qquad Z_L = j\omega L, \qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C}$$

The magnitude \(|Z|\) gives the ratio of amplitudes (in ohms); the angle gives the phase shift between voltage and current. Series impedances add; parallel ones combine like parallel resistors. Chapter 3 builds filters from this.

A component has impedance \(Z = 3 + j4\ \Omega\). What is its magnitude \(|Z|\)?
\(\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\ \Omega\), and the angle is \(\operatorname{atan}(4/3) \approx 53°\): the voltage leads the current by 53°, so the component is inductive. A 1 V amplitude sine drives 0.2 A amplitude through it.
What is the magnitude of the impedance of a 1 µF capacitor at 1 kHz?
\(|Z_C| = 1/(2\pi f C)\).
\(1/(2\pi\times1000\times10^{-6}) = 159.2\ \Omega\). At 100 kHz it's 1.59 Ω and at 1 MHz 0.16 Ω, which is why capacitors short out high-frequency noise (decoupling).
Multiplying a complex number by \(j\) rotates its arrow counter-clockwise by how many degrees? (A number.)
\(j\) itself is the unit arrow pointing straight up, at angle 90°, and multiplying adds angles.
90°. Do it twice and you've turned 180°, pointing the opposite way: that's \(j^2 = -1\). This is why an inductor's voltage (\(j\omega L\,I\)) leads its current by 90°.

Vectors, dot and cross products

Fields and forces have direction as well as size, so they're vectors: \(\vec a = (a_x, a_y, a_z)\). Adding vectors adds components. Two kinds of multiplication matter in electromagnetism, and each has a clear geometric meaning.

The dot product \(\vec a\cdot\vec b = a_xb_x + a_yb_y + a_zb_z = |a||b|\cos\theta\) is a number: how much of one vector lies along the other. It's largest when they're parallel, zero when perpendicular and negative when opposed. Work is a dot product, \(W = \vec F\cdot\vec d\), and so is voltage: \(V = -\int\vec E\cdot d\vec\ell\). Only the part of the field along your path counts.

The cross product \(\vec a\times\vec b\) is a vector perpendicular to both, with magnitude \(|a||b|\sin\theta\), the area of the parallelogram they span. Its direction follows the right-hand rule: fingers from \(\vec a\) toward \(\vec b\), thumb along \(\vec a\times\vec b\). It's largest when the vectors are perpendicular and zero when parallel. Electromagnetism is full of cross products: the magnetic force on a moving charge \(\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B\) (motors), the energy flow \(\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0\) (Chapter 1), and torque \(\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F\).

Compute \(\vec a\cdot\vec b\) for \(\vec a = (3, 4)\) and \(\vec b = (2, -1)\).
\(3\times2 + 4\times(-1) = 6 - 4 = 2\). It's positive but small, so the vectors are about 79° apart (\(\cos\theta = 2/(5\sqrt5)\)).
A wire carries current in the \(+x\) direction through a magnetic field pointing in \(+y\). The force on the moving positive charges is along \(\vec v\times\vec B\). Which way is the force on the wire?
  1. \(+z\)
  2. \(-z\)
  3. \(+y\)
  4. There is no force; current and field are perpendicular
\(\hat x\times\hat y = \hat z\). The force is perpendicular to both the current and the field, and largest when they are perpendicular. This is the force that turns every electric motor.

Node equations as a matrix

Kirchhoff's current law at every node of a circuit gives one linear equation per unknown node voltage. Linear equations are what matrices are for. Take a two-node circuit: a source \(V_s\) feeds node 1 through \(R_1\); \(R_2\) connects node 1 to ground; \(R_3\) connects node 1 to node 2; and \(R_4\) connects node 2 to ground. Write "currents leaving each node sum to zero", using conductances \(G = 1/R\):

$$\text{node 1:}\quad G_1(v_1 - V_s) + G_2 v_1 + G_3(v_1 - v_2) = 0$$ $$\text{node 2:}\quad G_3(v_2 - v_1) + G_4 v_2 = 0$$

Collect the terms for each unknown:

$$\begin{pmatrix} G_1 + G_2 + G_3 & -G_3 \\ -G_3 & G_3 + G_4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} G_1 V_s \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

\(\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{i}\). The conductance matrix has a simple pattern: each diagonal entry sums the conductances touching that node, and each off-diagonal entry is minus the conductance joining the two nodes.

Because of that pattern, a program can build the matrix by "stamping" each component into it: a resistor between nodes \(a\) and \(b\) adds \(G\) to entries \((a,a)\) and \((b,b)\) and subtracts \(G\) from \((a,b)\) and \((b,a)\). Then a single Gaussian elimination solves for all node voltages at once. That's precisely what this book's W.solveDC does, with one extension (modified nodal analysis): each voltage source adds an extra row and column for its unknown current. Diodes are handled by guessing on/off, solving, checking and repeating. SPICE works the same way, re-stamping a linearised version of each nonlinear part on every iteration.

// Gaussian elimination (no pivoting shown, for clarity)
for (let c = 0; c < n; c++)
  for (let r = c + 1; r < n; r++) {
    const f = A[r][c] / A[c][c];
    for (let k = c; k < n; k++) A[r][k] -= f * A[c][k];
    b[r] -= f * b[c];
  }
for (let r = n - 1; r >= 0; r--) {         // back-substitution
  let s = b[r];
  for (let k = r + 1; k < n; k++) s -= A[r][k] * x[k];
  x[r] = s / A[r][r];
}
With \(V_s = 10\) V and all four resistors equal to 1 kΩ in the circuit of Figure A.12, what is \(v_2\)?
With \(G = 1\) mS everywhere: \(3v_1 - v_2 = 10\) and \(-v_1 + 2v_2 = 0\). Substitute \(v_1 = 2v_2\).
\(v_1 = 2v_2\), so \(6v_2 - v_2 = 10\), giving \(v_2 = 2\) V and \(v_1 = 4\) V. Check with KCL at node 1: 6 mA in through \(R_1\), 4 mA out through \(R_2\), 2 mA out through \(R_3\). ✓
